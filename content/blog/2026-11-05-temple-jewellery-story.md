Few styles of Indian jewellery carry as much meaning as temple jewellery. Its name comes from its origins: ornaments created to adorn the deities in the temples of South India. Over centuries the same designs moved from the sanctum to the stage, where classical dancers wore them, and then into family collections and bridal trousseaus. Today temple jewellery is worn far beyond its region of origin, yet it still carries a sense of ceremony that few other styles match.

## From the sanctum to the stage

In many South Indian temples, the deities were dressed and adorned for festivals and daily rituals. The jewellery made for them was generous in scale and rich in symbolism, crafted to be seen from a distance in lamp-lit halls. Classical dance traditions such as Bharatanatyam later adopted similar ornaments, because the dancer often portrays deities and stories from the epics. The stage gave these designs a wider audience, and families began to commission them for weddings and celebrations.

> Temple jewellery was first made to honour the divine. That origin is still visible in every motif, every curve of the frame and every row of hanging drops.

## The motifs you will recognise

Temple jewellery uses a vocabulary of symbols that appear again and again. Once you know them, you will spot them everywhere:

- **Lakshmi**, the goddess of prosperity, often seated on a lotus at the centre of a pendant
- **Lotus flowers**, standing for purity and new beginnings
- **Peacocks**, associated with grace and beauty and with several deities
- **Mango or paisley shapes**, a symbol of fertility and abundance
- **Temple towers and arches**, echoing the architecture of the shrines themselves
- **Coin designs**, a reminder of prosperity and of old trade routes

Many pieces combine several motifs, with a deity framed by an arch, flanked by peacocks and finished with a fringe of small drops.

## How temple jewellery is made

### Repoussé and nakshi work

Much of the detail in temple jewellery is raised from sheet metal. In repoussé, the craftsperson works the metal from the back to push the design forward, then refines it from the front. Nakshi work, often used for figurative panels, involves chasing and carving fine details by hand so that faces, garments and flowers stand out in relief. It is slow work, and the depth of the relief is one of the clearest signs of a skilled hand.

### Stones and finishing

Traditional pieces were often set with rubies and emeralds, or with coloured stones chosen for their deep red and green tones. Pearls are used in drops and borders. The finish is usually a warm, slightly antique gold tone that suits the figurative details and photographs beautifully under warm light.

## The key pieces

A full temple set can include many ornaments, but these are the ones you will see most often:

1. **Haaram**: a long necklace, often with a large central pendant.
2. **Kasu mala**: a necklace made of rows of coin motifs.
3. **Choker or addigai**: a short, close-fitting necklace.
4. **Jhumkas**: bell-shaped earrings, often with pearl or stone drops.
5. **Vanki**: an armlet shaped to sit on the upper arm.
6. **Oddiyanam**: a waist belt, especially in bridal sets.
7. **Nethi chutti**: an ornament worn along the hair parting and on the forehead.

## Wearing temple jewellery today

Temple jewellery no longer has to mean a full bridal set. Modern ways to wear it include:

- a single statement pendant on a simple chain with a plain silk or cotton saree
- temple jhumkas with a contemporary dress or kurta
- a choker layered with a delicate longer chain
- a coin necklace as the one ornate piece in an otherwise minimal look

Let one piece lead. Because the designs are so detailed, a single ornament often says more than a full set.

## Caring for temple pieces

The raised details that make temple jewellery special can also trap dust and moisture. Store each piece flat in its own lined box, away from humidity. Wipe gently with a soft, dry cloth after wearing and avoid perfume, water and chemical cleaners. For deep cleaning or loose stones, take the piece to a jeweller rather than soaking it at home. Temple jewellery is more than an accessory. It carries centuries of devotion, craft and celebration, and wearing it is a small way of keeping that story alive.
