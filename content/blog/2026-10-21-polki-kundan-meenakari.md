Polki, kundan and meenakari are names you will hear in almost every conversation about traditional Indian jewellery. They are often mentioned together, and many pieces combine all three, but they describe different things. Polki is a kind of stone, kundan is a way of setting stones, and meenakari is the art of enamelling. Understanding the difference helps you appreciate what you are looking at and ask the right questions before you buy.

## Polki: diamonds in their natural form

Polki refers to uncut diamonds. Instead of being cut into many reflective facets like a modern brilliant diamond, a polki stone keeps a flatter, more natural shape with only minimal shaping and polishing. The result is a softer, glowing sparkle rather than a bright flash.

Because each polki stone has its own shape, pieces are designed around the stones rather than the other way round. That is one reason why no two polki pieces are exactly alike.

### What to look for

- A flat or gently domed surface with a soft, mirror-like glow
- Irregular outlines that follow the stone's natural shape
- A foil backing in many traditional pieces, which reflects light back through the stone

## Kundan: the art of setting

Kundan is a setting technique. The word refers to highly refined gold, which is worked into thin strips and pressed around the stone to hold it in place. Unlike claw or prong settings, kundan surrounds the stone with a smooth gold frame.

The technique has long been associated with the royal courts of Rajasthan and the Mughal period, and Jaipur and Bikaner remain known for it today. Kundan settings can hold polki, coloured stones or glass, which is why it is important to ask what kind of stone is set in a kundan piece.

> Kundan describes how a stone is held. Polki describes what the stone is. A piece can be polki set in kundan, or kundan-set with other stones entirely.

## Meenakari: colour in enamel

Meenakari is the art of decorating metal with coloured enamel. The craftsperson engraves small hollows into the metal, fills them with coloured enamel powder and fires the piece so the enamel melts and fuses. Colours are applied and fired one at a time, often starting with those that need the highest heat.

Jaipur is particularly known for meenakari, with its rich reds, greens and blues on a gold ground. Very often meenakari decorates the back of a kundan or polki piece, so the reverse is as beautiful as the front. Turn a traditional necklace over and you may find birds, flowers and vines in bright enamel.

## How the three come together

A traditional bridal piece might use all three:

1. A design is shaped in gold and the back is engraved.
2. The back is enamelled in meenakari, colour by colour.
3. The stones, often polki, are set on the front using the kundan technique.
4. Pearls, drops and threads are added to finish the piece.

This layered process is why such pieces take time to make and why they are often handed down as heirlooms.

## Questions worth asking before you buy

- What kind of stones are set in the piece: uncut diamonds, coloured gemstones or glass?
- What metal and purity is the setting?
- Is the enamel hand-applied meenakari?
- How should it be stored and cleaned?

A reputable jeweller will answer clearly, and the answers help you compare pieces fairly.

## Caring for polki, kundan and meenakari

These pieces need gentle care:

- Store flat in a lined box, separated from other jewellery, to protect enamel and settings.
- Keep away from water, perfume and creams; moisture can affect foil backings.
- Wipe with a soft, dry cloth after wearing. Do not soak or use ultrasonic cleaners.
- Have settings checked by a jeweller if a stone feels loose.

Learning to recognise these three techniques changes how you look at Indian jewellery. Each piece becomes a record of several crafts working together, and a little understanding makes it even more rewarding to wear.
